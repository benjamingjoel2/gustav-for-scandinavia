It is the question we are asked most often, usually in the first email. Summer or winter? Light that never leaves, or light that might arrive for twenty minutes after midnight? There is no correct answer. There is only the one that suits you, and it helps to know what each season actually feels like on the ground.

## Two seasons, one latitude

North of the Arctic Circle the year is lopsided. In summer the sun stays above the horizon for weeks. In winter, in the far north, it doesn't rise at all. Between those extremes sit two shorter shoulder seasons that many people overlook, and which can be the most rewarding of all.

The dates move with latitude. In Tromsø the midnight sun runs from roughly 18 May to 25 July. At the North Cape it is about 12 May to 31 July. On Svalbard, much further north, it lasts from around 19 April to 24 August. In Rovaniemi, right on the Arctic Circle, the window is short: roughly 6 June to 5 July. Our [light calendar](/light-calendar) shows these side by side.

The northern lights need darkness, so their season runs from late September to late March. The aurora is there in summer too. You simply can't see it against a bright sky.

## What the midnight sun is like

In June, Tromsø forgets to get dark. You'll forget to sleep. The first night is disorientating; by the third, most people stop looking at the clock. Hikes start at nine in the evening. Dinner runs late because there is no reason for it not to. The light at midnight is low and golden, and it lasts for hours rather than minutes.

What it is good for: walking, kayaking, long drives on empty coast roads, island hopping in [Lofoten](/destinations/lofoten), sitting outside. Everything is open, ferries run their full timetables, and mountain trails are free of snow from about late June, depending on altitude and the year.

What it is less good for: sleep, if you are sensitive to light. Most hotels have blackout curtains, but not all do it well. Bring an eye mask. Summer is also the busiest time on the coast, so places worth staying in book early.

Weather is mild rather than warm. Expect around 10 to 15°C in northern Norway, sometimes more, sometimes considerably less, with rain that comes and goes. Pack layers and a proper waterproof.

## What aurora season is like

We don't promise the northern lights. We put you where your chances are best. That means dark skies away from town lights, a few nights rather than one, and a guide who reads the forecasts and is willing to drive towards clear weather.

The aurora depends on two things you can't control: solar activity and cloud cover. Of the two, cloud matters more. A strong display behind thick cloud is invisible. A modest one under a clear sky can be very good. Inland areas such as [Abisko and Swedish Lapland](/destinations/swedish-lapland) or [Finnish Lapland](/destinations/finnish-lapland) tend to have more stable, drier weather than the coast. The coast, around [Tromsø](/destinations/tromso), is milder and has other things to do during the day.

Winter brings its own rewards: dog sledding, snowshoeing, sauna and cold water, the blue light of the short days. From roughly late November to mid January, Tromsø sees no sunrise at all. It is not pitch black. There are a few hours of soft twilight around midday, in colours that are hard to describe and harder to photograph.

It is also cold. On the coast, temperatures often hover around a few degrees below zero. Inland Lapland can drop to -30°C on clear nights. Good clothing makes the difference between enduring it and enjoying it.

## The shoulder seasons

Late September and early October are underrated. The nights are dark enough for aurora, the lakes have not frozen, and the birch forests turn yellow and red. In Lapland the autumn colours, ruska, peak around early to mid September. You can see the northern lights reflected in still water, which is not something winter offers.

March is the other one. Days are long again, there is still deep snow, and the aurora season has a few weeks left. It is often the most comfortable month for winter activities, simply because you have daylight to do them in.

## How to choose

A few honest questions help.

**Do you want to be active outdoors for long days?** Summer. The light gives you time, and the trails are open.

**Is seeing the aurora the main reason for the trip?** Winter or the shoulder months, and plan at least three or four nights in a dark-sky location. More nights means better odds. There are no guarantees either way.

**Are you travelling with young children?** Summer is easier, though winter works well with the right clothing and shorter outings. Late bedtimes in June are less of a problem than you might think.

**Do you dislike the cold?** Then autumn, not January. Late September gives you a real chance of aurora at temperatures most people find comfortable.

**Do you want to avoid crowds?** The shoulder seasons, again. Late August, September, March.

## Doing both

Some people come twice. Others build a single journey around the transition, for example a trip in September that starts with long, golden evenings in Lofoten and ends with the first properly dark nights in Lapland. The aurora is not a sure thing that early, but it is a reasonable bet.
