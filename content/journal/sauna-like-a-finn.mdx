Sauna, cold water, repeat. Locals call it maintenance.

Finland has a population of about five and a half million and, by most estimates, millions of saunas. There are saunas in apartment blocks, offices, summer cottages, gyms and even the parliament building. Finnish sauna culture is on UNESCO's list of intangible cultural heritage. None of this makes it formal or complicated. It is a place to get warm, get clean and slow down. A few habits make it more pleasant for everyone.

## Before you go in

Shower first. This is the one rule nobody bends. Wash properly, without a swimsuit if the sauna is single-sex or private, and step in clean.

Swimwear varies by place. In private saunas and at home, people go without. In public saunas the rules depend on the venue: many mixed public saunas ask for swimwear, while single-sex sections usually don't. If in doubt, look at what others are doing or ask at the desk. Nobody minds the question.

Take a towel or a small seat cover to sit on. It is a matter of hygiene, and often the wooden benches are hot enough that you'll want it anyway.

Leave your phone outside. Heat is bad for it, and so is photographing people in a sauna.

## Inside

Higher benches are hotter. If it's your first time, start low and move up if you are comfortable. There is no prize for staying longest.

Löyly is the Finnish word for the steam that rises when water is thrown on the hot stones. It is also, more loosely, the feeling of the heat itself. Someone will usually have the ladle. If you would like more löyly, it is polite to ask the room before you throw water. If you would like less, it is fine to say so or to move down a bench.

Conversation is welcome, quietly. Finns are often described as silent in the sauna, and some are. Others talk about work, weather, the summer cottage. What you won't find is loud talk, bragging or anyone trying to impress. Sauna is not a party, and it is not a place to show off. Endurance contests are frowned upon, and for good reason.

Ten minutes is a reasonable first round. Leave when you feel you have had enough, not when you think you should.

## Cooling down

This is the part people remember. Between rounds you cool off, ideally in a lake or the sea, otherwise in a cold shower or plunge pool. In winter, at some lakeside saunas, there is a hole cut in the ice, an avanto. You go down the ladder, stay in for a few seconds, and come back up feeling unusually awake.

It is not compulsory. A cool shower and a few minutes sitting outside does the same job. If you have a heart condition or high blood pressure, check with a doctor before trying the ice hole, and never go in alone.

Then back into the heat. Two or three rounds is typical. Some people do more. Drink water between rounds. A cold beer or a glass of juice afterwards is traditional, but not inside the sauna and not in excess.

## Smoke sauna

The savusauna is the older form. There is no chimney. A large stove is fired for hours, often four to six, filling the room with smoke. When the stones are hot enough, the smoke is let out and the room left to settle. What remains is a soft, deep heat and a faint smell of wood smoke that stays on your skin for the rest of the day.

Because it takes so long to prepare, a smoke sauna is usually heated only on particular days, and it is worth planning around. In the [Finnish Lakeland](/destinations/lakeland) and in [Lapland](/destinations/finnish-lapland), some small lakeside places heat theirs once or twice a week. We find out when and build the day around it.

## The vihta

In summer you may see bundles of fresh birch twigs, called vihta in western Finland and vasta in the east. You soak the bundle and use it to lightly beat your skin in the heat. It smells of birch leaves and improves circulation. It sounds odd and feels very pleasant. If one is offered, try it.

## Sauna beyond Finland

Sauna is common across the region, and in recent years public saunas have appeared along the waterfronts of Oslo, Stockholm, Copenhagen and many smaller towns, often floating or built on piers, with a ladder straight into the harbour. The etiquette is broadly the same. Shower first, sit on a towel, keep it calm, cool off in the water.

In [Helsinki](/destinations/helsinki) there are traditional public saunas that have been running for decades and newer ones by the sea. Both are worth visiting. A neighbourhood sauna on a weekday evening, full of regulars, tells you more about the city than most museums.

## In short

Shower. Towel. Start low. Ask before adding löyly. Talk quietly or not at all. Cool off. Repeat. Drink water. That is most of it. The rest you learn by doing it often, which is rather the idea.
