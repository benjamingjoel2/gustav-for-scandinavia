More people ask us to plan journeys without flights. Some want to cut their emissions. Some simply prefer arriving overland, watching the landscape change rather than skipping it. Both are good reasons. Here is what is possible, what is easy, and where it gets difficult.

## Getting here

If you live in continental Europe or the UK, reaching Scandinavia by rail is entirely realistic. Germany is the gateway. From Hamburg, direct trains run to [Copenhagen](/destinations/copenhagen) in about five hours, give or take, depending on the route and current engineering works. Hamburg itself is well connected to Amsterdam, Brussels, Paris (via Cologne or Frankfurt), Berlin and much of central Europe.

From London, the usual route is Eurostar to Brussels or Paris, onward to Cologne or Hamburg, and then north. It can be done in a long day, but most people break it with a night in Hamburg, Cologne or Amsterdam. That night is not wasted. It is part of the trip.

There are also seasonal and year-round night trains from parts of Germany and the Netherlands towards Copenhagen and Stockholm. Timetables change, and some services run only part of the year, so we check what is running for your dates rather than promising a particular train.

If you are coming from North America, Asia or Australia, you will fly to reach Europe. We can't change that, and we won't pretend otherwise. What we can do is make the rest of the journey overland, which still removes a meaningful share of what you would otherwise fly.

## Crossing the region by train

Once you are in Copenhagen, the Nordic rail network does a lot of the work.

**Copenhagen to Stockholm** takes about five to five and a half hours by direct train, across the Øresund Bridge and through southern Sweden.

**Copenhagen or Stockholm to Oslo** takes roughly five to eight hours depending on the route. Gothenburg is a natural stop on the way.

**Oslo to Bergen** takes about six and a half to seven hours across the Hardangervidda plateau. We have written about it separately in our journal, and we recommend it without reservation.

**Stockholm to Narvik** runs as a night train, heading north through forest and then above the Arctic Circle, past Kiruna and [Abisko](/destinations/swedish-lapland), and down to the Norwegian coast at Narvik. You fall asleep in the Swedish forest and wake up in Lapland. From Narvik, buses connect onward towards [Lofoten](/destinations/lofoten) and [Tromsø](/destinations/tromso).

**Helsinki to Rovaniemi** is another night train, carrying you from the Finnish capital to the Arctic Circle overnight. In winter it is a practical way to reach [Finnish Lapland](/destinations/finnish-lapland) for aurora season. Some services carry cars as well.

Sleeper cabins range from simple couchettes to private compartments with their own washbasin or shower. They book up, especially in December and around holidays. We book early.

## Crossing by sea

Ferries fill the gaps the railways leave.

**Copenhagen to Oslo** runs overnight. You leave in the afternoon, sail up the Øresund and the Kattegat, and arrive in Oslo the next morning, coming up the Oslofjord.

**Stockholm to Helsinki or Turku** are overnight crossings through the archipelagos. Thousands of low, rocky islands line the way out of Stockholm and in towards Turku, and on a light summer evening you see a good part of them from the deck. Several operators run these routes.

**Hirtshals to the Faroe Islands** is a longer crossing from the top of Denmark, typically a day and a half or so, depending on the season and schedule. It is the main way to reach the [Faroe Islands](/destinations/faroe-islands) without flying. It is weather-dependent, and the North Atlantic can be rough. Good sailors enjoy it. Others should plan for a quiet day.

One honest note. Ferries are not automatically low-carbon. Large overnight ferries carry a lot of weight, and their emissions per passenger vary a great deal depending on the vessel, how full it is and how much freight it carries. Coastal voyages and cruises along the Norwegian coast are a lovely way to travel, but they are not a low-carbon choice, and we don't present them as one. More on this on our [travelling lighter](/travelling-lighter) page.

## Where flying is still the answer

Svalbard. There is no regular passenger ferry or practical overland route to Longyearbyen. Scheduled flights from Oslo or Tromsø are the only sensible way to get there. If Svalbard is on your list, we will plan it with a flight and say so plainly.

The far north of Norway, such as Kirkenes and the North Cape, can be reached overland, but it takes time: long bus journeys, or days on the coastal route. Sometimes a single domestic flight on a long journey is the reasonable compromise, and we will tell you what the trade-off looks like rather than decide for you.

## What it costs, in time

A flight-free journey is slower. A route that takes three flights might take six or seven days overland. We don't think of that as a cost so much as the shape of the trip. Fewer, longer stays. More time looking out of windows. Arriving somewhere and knowing how far you have come.

It is also less fragile than people expect. Missed connections happen, but in our experience they are usually easier to recover from on the ground than in an airport.
