Much of the Arctic north that visitors come to see, the tundra, the reindeer, the long winter light, is Sápmi. It helps to know a little about the place and the people before you arrive. This page is a starting point, not the full picture.

## Sápmi and the Sámi

Sápmi is the traditional homeland of the Sámi people. It stretches across the north of Norway, Sweden and Finland, and into the Kola peninsula in Russia. It has no national borders, but it is a real place with its own history and communities.

The Sámi are an Indigenous people. There are several Sámi languages, not one, and some of them are spoken by only a few hundred people. Norway, Sweden and Finland each have a Sámi parliament: the Sámediggi in Norway, the Sametinget in Sweden and the Saamelaiskäräjät in Finland. They work on language, culture, land use and the rights of Sámi people in each country.

Sámi culture is a living culture. Most Sámi people today live modern lives in towns and cities, work in every kind of job, and are as likely to be lawyers, teachers or engineers as reindeer herders. Sámi music, film, literature, design and politics are active and contemporary. It is worth setting aside any expectation of a historical exhibit. You are visiting a present-day homeland, not a museum.

The history includes a long period of assimilation policies in all of the Nordic countries, including pressure to give up languages and traditional ways of life. Its effects are still felt.

## Why choose Sámi-owned experiences

Tourism in the north often uses Sámi imagery without involving Sámi people. Reindeer, tents and patterned clothing appear in brochures for businesses that have no connection to the community.

When you choose an experience that is Sámi-owned and Sámi-led, the money stays with the community, and so does the storytelling. You hear about herding, language and history from people who live it, in the way they choose to tell it.

It is not always obvious from a website who owns what. The simplest approach is to ask the operator directly whether they are Sámi-owned. A good operator will answer plainly. We ask the same question of everyone we work with.

## How reindeer herding works

Reindeer herding is a livelihood, not a show. In Norway and Sweden, it is largely reserved for Sámi people by law. In Finland, herding is also practised by non-Sámi residents in the herding area, though it remains central to Sámi culture there too.

Every reindeer you meet in Sápmi has an owner. They are not wild animals, even when they wander across the road far from any farm. Each is marked with an ear notch that identifies its owner. (Norway's few herds of wild reindeer live much further south, in mountain areas such as the Hardangervidda.)

Herding follows the seasons. Herds move between winter pastures, often inland in the forest or on the plateau, and summer pastures, often on the coast or in the mountains. These migrations can cover long distances and are planned around the weather, the snow and the condition of the animals.

Spring calving, roughly in May, is a sensitive time. Calves are vulnerable, and disturbance can separate them from their mothers. Autumn brings gathering and marking, and winter is about finding grazing under the snow, which is becoming harder as the climate changes and winters become less predictable.

A visit to a herding family is a visit to a working operation. Timings depend on the animals and the weather.

## What not to do

A few things cause real harm or offence, often without the visitor realising.

**Don't wear gákti as a costume.** The gákti is traditional Sámi clothing. Its colours and patterns can show where a person comes from and which family they belong to. Wearing an imitation for a photo or a party is not appropriate.

**Don't photograph people or their homes without asking.** This includes people in gákti at markets or events. Ask first, and accept no as an answer.

**Don't approach or feed reindeer herds.** Keep your distance, even if the animals seem calm. Don't fly drones near reindeer; they can panic and scatter a herd. Keep dogs on a lead, especially in spring when the calves are young.

**Don't buy fake "Sámi" souvenirs.** Mass-produced items using Sámi patterns are common in tourist shops. For genuine Sámi handicraft, look for the Sámi Duodji label, which marks work made by Sámi craftspeople.

**Don't ask for a joik as entertainment.** Joik is a traditional Sámi form of song, often personal, sometimes about a specific person, place or animal. It is not a performance on request. If you are offered one, listen.

**Don't assume.** Not every Sámi person herds reindeer, lives in a lávvu or wants to talk about their identity with strangers. Let people tell you what they choose to.
